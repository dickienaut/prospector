# Project Prospector

### Detecting secular technological shifts as they form — a research note on an early-stage instrument

*2026-07-02. Written as an overview for a technical reader. The system is early: its core detector has passed a first cheap validation; most of the apparatus is still to be built. What follows is an honest account of the idea, the one empirical result so far, and the many things we do not yet know.*

---

## The problem it is built around

The setting is a single long-horizon investor — a 17+ year holding period, a wish for durable exposure to emerging technology through what looks like a period of unusually rapid technological change, and a hard-won belief that his own *over-trading* is the primary enemy. The tool is therefore designed less to find opportunities than to find them *rarely and well*, and to displace trading rather than enable it.

The central intellectual problem is what we've been calling the **consensus trap**, and it's worth stating precisely because the whole design is a response to it. Any system that scans the world and ranks investment theses by the *quality* of their argument is, in effect, selecting on how thoroughly a thesis has been documented — and documentation is positively correlated with how completely the thesis is already reflected in prices. The better-evidenced the case, the more it has been written about, the more it is priced in. Taken to its limit, a web-scanning thesis-ranker is a beautiful machine for discovering *what everyone already knows*. Argument quality is not mispricing, and worse, it is adversely selected against it.

Two moves resolve this for this particular owner:

1. **The horizon dissolves the trap.** A 17-year owner does not need mispricing or alpha; he needs durable, real, secular shifts he can own for two decades. "Consensus-but-true" is entirely acceptable — a shift can be widely recognized and still be a substrate worth holding for twenty years. That alone defuses the trap, because it stops the tool from pretending to measure a mispricing it cannot see.

2. **Change the target from quality to novelty.** Instead of ranking arguments, detect *change as it forms* — which is, by construction, *before* consensus rather than after. The governing slogan is *novelty earns a look; reality earns a position.* A crucial distinction rides underneath: **novelty is not interest.** Novelty is a measurable departure from a baseline; interest is whatever excites the operator — which is simply confirmation bias wearing a more flattering name. The system is built to detect the former and to be actively suspicious of the latter.

## The instrument

The organizing idea is that **an ETF is a pre-packaged thesis.** The universe of exchange-traded funds is a maintained, curated ontology handed to you for free: each fund is a holdings list (the entities), a mandate (the explicit thesis), a rebalance schedule (a professional curator continuously updating that thesis), and an issuer with a commercial stake in drawing the boundary well. This converts a vague "scan the discourse" problem into a tractable "watch a structured universe of baskets" problem.

Those baskets produce signals at different points on the journey from frontier to consensus, and **the object we actually want to measure is the lag between them**:

- **Discourse** (earliest, noisiest) — a linkage beginning to be discussed;
- **Rebalancing / holdings** (middle, curated) — a professional quietly acting on it;
- **Launches** (near-consensus) — someone packaging it into a fund, which is a dated, public timestamp of the moment a thesis became "package-able."

There is a clean empirical anchor here that your kind of reader will appreciate. Ben-David, Franzoni, Kim & Moussawi (*Review of Financial Studies*, 2023, "Competition for Attention in the ETF Space") document that specialized ETFs tend to launch *just after the peak of excitement* about a theme and subsequently underperform by roughly 30% in risk-adjusted terms over five years — not explained by fees, and driven by overvaluation of the underlying stocks at launch. We are proposing to use, as *instrumentation*, precisely the phenomenon that paper documents as investor harm: the launch is a consensus clock. The people who *buy* the timestamp lose; we only read it. More broadly, none of the components is new — the design draws on category comovement beyond fundamentals (Barberis, Shleifer & Wurgler, 2005), correlation-network structure in markets (Mantegna, 1999), and burst detection in text streams (Kleinberg, 2002). The contribution, if there is one, is the *assembly*: three streams ordered on a consensus curve, with the inter-stream lag as the measured quantity, sitting on a record that scores its own past forecasts. That the pieces have prior art is reassuring rather than discouraging — a wholly unprecedented idea in this space would more likely be wrong than brilliant.

The **v1 core that is actually being built is entirely deterministic** and runs on free price and holdings data — no machine learning, no language models, no discretionary judgment inside the loop. It has three lead-spawning detectors:

- **Inter-basket excess correlation** — the residual correlation between two sector ETFs *after stripping the common market factor* (a rolling market-beta regression), measured as a departure from its own trailing-year baseline. This catches two sectors beginning to move together beyond what their shared market exposure explains — the signature of a new linkage forming.
- **Intra-basket dispersion** — a sector's own constituents fracturing into distinct sub-clusters, which is the signature of a category under strain, a taxonomy starting to mis-fit reality (the way "Communication Services" now holds both Alphabet and legacy telecoms that no longer move alike).
- **Launch detection** — new funds as consensus timestamps.

Wrapped around the detectors is a discipline layer that is, honestly, the part that makes the project unusual, and it exists because the designer treats his own impulses as the adversary. The record is **append-only and immutable** — an event-sourced "fossil record" in which nothing is ever edited, so both the machine's signals and the human's verdicts are permanently and honestly scoreable, and hindsight cannot quietly rewrite the past to make an outcome look inevitable. The output is **a reading list, never an order** — the system proposes attention and never executes. There is an explicit **throughput ceiling**: because the scarce resource is human adjudication, the system is tuned to surface *little*, and over-firing is treated as the tool's characteristic failure mode. And the cadence is deliberately **anti-engagement** — weekly, with no compulsively-checkable surfaces — because a tool built to reduce trading must not itself become a slot machine.

## What the first test showed

The first real validation (what we call the milestone-3 gate) replayed the excess-correlation detector over six years of actual end-of-day prices (2019–2024), across all 55 pairs of the eleven S&P sector ETFs. Its purpose was narrow and cheap: establish whether the instrument can see a *known* past structural shift form, *before* building four more subsystems on top of it. Four findings, stated as honestly as we can:

**1. Recall — confirmed.** The Tech–Utilities pair (XLK×XLU) departs its baseline on **2023-01-25** (departure z = 2.52), well before "AI needs enormous amounts of power" became a market cliché. The detector sees Tech and Utilities decouple from the broad market and re-couple to *each other* — the structural fingerprint of the data-center power build-out — while it was still forming.

**2. The placebo test was run first, and passed.** Before any threshold was tuned, we checked the thing that could invalidate everything: does the market-beta strip actually remove the common factor, or do all sectors merely co-move in a crash (which would make every "signal" a spurious artifact of market-wide panic)? Mean pairwise *residual* correlation collapses to approximately zero in every window, **including the March-2020 crash** (raw correlation 0.88 → residual −0.035). So the many pairs that cross threshold over six years are genuine idiosyncratic departures, not beta leakage, and the firings do not cluster in stress windows. This settled the key methodological fork: the remaining work is *gating and surfacing discipline*, not the estimator. Running the null test before the calibration, rather than after, is the discipline we're proudest of.

**3. An instructive miscalibration — and the discipline around fixing it.** A placeholder "market-stress gate," meant to suppress signals during genuine crises, was set at a raw-correlation level of 0.65. But sector ETFs are *structurally* correlated (~0.5–0.65 even in calm times), so 0.65 gated nearly a quarter of all trading days and, in particular, suppressed the real 2023 signal above. The consequence was severe: the first *recorded* firing slipped to late 2024, and the measured lead time inverted to **−58 days** — the tool appeared to *trail* the ETF launch, i.e. to be useless. Re-specifying the gate to its correct, narrow job (crisis-only, and measured as a departure from the correlation regime's own trailing norm rather than an absolute level) recovers a lead time of roughly **nineteen months ahead** of the fund's existence — which is the entire thesis. The point we are most careful about: this recalibration is authorized because a *proof* (the beta-strip diagnostic) redefined what the gate is *for* — not because tuning made the answer prettier. The recalibrated gate is validated against the *concept* (does it fire in genuine crises and stay quiet on ordinary calm days?), never against the single known case. Tuning a threshold until one labeled example lights up is curve-fitting to n = 1, and we are treating the exciting nineteen-month result as something to *observe as a consequence*, never as an objective to optimize toward.

**4. Precision is honestly unmeasurable right now.** Of 98 firings over the window, only 2 auto-score as "structural" — but this is a floor artifact, not a verdict. The corroboration set contains only three verified fund launches, all from 2024, so almost nothing that fired before mid-2024 *can* be corroborated yet; the two that do are exactly the right AI-power-cluster pairs. There is a deeper limit worth naming: the auto-scorer treats "eventually packaged into a fund" as the proxy for "real," which is a *lagging and incomplete* proxy — a genuine shift the market never wraps in a fund would score as junk. So the human verdict remains ground truth and the automated score is a *witness, not a judge* — a number we have explicitly forbidden ourselves from optimizing. (A small illustration of the intended culture: adversarial review caught a bug in which the two-character token "ai" was matching *inside* words like "retail" and "entertainment," inflating the structural count from a true 2 to a false 8; it was corrected downward and reported against our own interest.)

## What we don't yet know, and what we're still building

The honest frontier is large, and some of it is exactly where an economist and data scientist would have the sharpest opinions:

- **The permanent epistemic ceiling.** This is a read-only fossil record. Unlike a controlled experiment — the biological analogy we work from is Lenski's long-term evolution experiment, where you can thaw a frozen ancestor and *re-run* evolution to prove causation — we can never re-run history. The system can reconstruct the *path* a shift took; it can never *demonstrate the cause*. "Reconstructed path, never demonstrated cause" is written into the metric on purpose, because a read-only record is dangerously good at letting you draw an elegant inevitable line backward from any outcome and mistake it for a law.

- **n = 1.** We have validated against essentially one known shift. Success on a single case is weak evidence — it *fails to refute* the approach rather than confirming it. The real test is prospective and accumulates only as more launches occur and more shifts form. We are deliberately not overfitting to the one example we can see.

- **Genuinely statistical open questions.** Overlapping estimation windows autocorrelate, so the z-scores overstate significance and our thresholds are empirical rather than derived from a Gaussian null. Whether a single market factor is sufficient, or whether a multi-factor strip is needed at crisis onset (where a stale trailing beta briefly leaks), is open. And there is a real multiple-comparisons / false-discovery problem across many pairs and a very low, poorly-characterized base rate of true "structural shift" events — the surfacing thresholds are set on principle now and can only be validated live as evidence accumulates. This is precisely the territory where we'd welcome an expert's skepticism.

- **What's still unbuilt.** The *discourse* layer — the earliest and most valuable signal, and by far the hardest, since it requires language models over news with all the hallucination risk that implies — is deliberately deferred. v1 *captures and freezes* raw discourse without interpreting it, so that a retrospective archive exists to mine later, without paying the expensive and error-prone interpretation cost up front. The dispersion detector needs survivorship-clean, point-in-time historical holdings data (a genuine data-availability question) and until it is calibrated it ships in "shadow mode" — recording but not alerting. And most of the surrounding system — the remaining detectors, the outputs, the deployment — is still ahead. What exists today is the fossil-record infrastructure, the fund-identity layer, and the excess-correlation detector with its acceptance test: about 262 tests, all running offline.

## Why it's interesting

Underneath the investing framing, this is as much a research question as a tool: *can secular structural change be detected as it forms — cheaply, from the topology of how markets and their financial packaging move — early enough to matter to a patient owner, and honestly enough to know when it is wrong?* The distinguishing property of the design is that it is engineered to be able to **lose**: falsifiable at every layer, with an immutable record that grades its own calls and a metric that refuses to flatter itself. That is an unusual stance for anything built around markets, and it is the property that makes the whole thing either worth taking seriously or worth cleanly refuting — which, either way, is the point.
